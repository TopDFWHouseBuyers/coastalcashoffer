Selling a House As-Is in California - What Sellers Need to Know

Published October 10, 2026 · Southern California
Back to All Articles

Selling a house as-is in California means you're offering your property in its current condition, without making repairs or renovations before the sale. Whether you're dealing with an inherited property, facing foreclosure, or simply want to avoid the hassle of fixing up your home, understanding the as-is selling process can save you time, money, and stress.

What Does 'As-Is' Really Mean in California?

When you sell a house as-is in California, you're essentially telling potential buyers that you won't be making any repairs or improvements before closing. The property transfers in its current condition, and buyers accept it with all existing issues—whether that's outdated plumbing, cosmetic damage, or structural concerns.

However, selling as-is doesn't mean you can hide problems. California law still requires full disclosure of known defects, which is an important distinction many sellers misunderstand.

California Disclosure Requirements You Can't Skip

Even when selling your house as-is, California's strict disclosure laws still apply. The state has some of the most comprehensive disclosure requirements in the nation, designed to protect buyers from hidden defects.

Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS)

California Civil Code Section 1102 requires sellers of most residential properties (1-4 units) to complete a Transfer Disclosure Statement. This document requires you to disclose any known material defects affecting the property's value or desirability. You must reveal issues with the foundation, roof, plumbing, electrical systems, HVAC, appliances, and any other problems you're aware of.

Natural Hazard Disclosure Statement

Southern California properties often face unique environmental risks. You'll need to disclose if your property is located in special hazard zones, including earthquake fault zones, flood hazard areas, fire hazard severity zones (particularly relevant in Orange County foothill areas like Yorba Linda), and seismic hazard zones. These disclosures are mandatory regardless of your property's condition.

Additional California-Specific Disclosures

Depending on your property's location and characteristics, you may need to provide additional disclosures about lead-based paint (for homes built before 1978), military ordnance locations, Mello-Roos taxes, homeowners association requirements, and death on the property within the past three years.

Failing to properly disclose known defects—even when selling as-is—can result in lawsuits and financial liability after the sale closes. Working with an experienced cash buyer who understands California's disclosure requirements can simplify this process considerably.

The Financial Reality of Selling As-Is in California

California's real estate market is one of the most expensive in the nation, with median home values in Orange County regularly exceeding $900,000. This high-value market creates unique considerations when selling as-is.

How Much Less Will You Get?

When listing as-is through traditional channels, sellers typically receive 10-30% less than comparable move-in ready homes, depending on the extent of needed repairs. However, when you factor in repair costs, carrying costs during a longer sale process, and real estate commissions (typically 5-6% in California), the net difference often narrows significantly.

Costs You'll Still Pay

Even selling as-is, you'll face several expenses in California. Property taxes are prorated through closing day, and at California's rates (around 1.1% of assessed value plus local assessments), this can be substantial. Title and escrow fees in California typically run $2,000-4,000 or more depending on the sale price. If you have an existing mortgage, you'll need to pay off the balance and any prepayment penalties. Outstanding HOA dues, liens, and transfer taxes must also be settled.

The Traditional As-Is Sale Process in California

If you list your as-is property with a real estate agent, the process typically unfolds over several months. You'll still need to prepare disclosures, allow showings and inspections, and navigate California's complex escrow process.

California's Escrow Timeline

California is an escrow state, meaning a neutral third party handles the transaction details. Traditional as-is sales typically take 30-60 days to close, sometimes longer if buyers need financing approval or if title issues arise. During this period, you're still responsible for property taxes, insurance, utilities, and maintenance.

Buyer Inspection Contingencies

Even with as-is sales, buyers typically conduct inspections. While you're not obligated to make repairs, inspection findings often lead to renegotiation attempts. Many deals fall apart during this phase when buyers get cold feet about the property's condition or request price reductions beyond the original agreement.

Financing Challenges

Properties with significant deferred maintenance often don't qualify for conventional financing. FHA and VA loans require homes to meet minimum property standards. If your as-is property doesn't qualify, you've eliminated a large portion of potential buyers, which can mean months on the market or ultimately accepting a lower offer from a cash buyer anyway.

Who Should Consider Selling As-Is?

Selling as-is makes sense for many Southern California homeowners in specific situations.

Inherited Properties

If you've inherited a house in Orange County or elsewhere in Southern California, you may live far away or lack the time and resources to manage renovations. As-is sales let you convert the inheritance to cash quickly without the burden of property management from a distance.

Financial Hardship or Foreclosure

Homeowners facing foreclosure, job loss, medical bills, or divorce often can't afford to invest thousands in repairs. Selling as-is—particularly to a cash buyer—can provide a fast exit and help you avoid foreclosure's credit damage.

Relocating for Work

California's job market is dynamic, and relocations happen frequently. If you're moving out of state or across the country for work, selling as-is eliminates the need to manage contractors and renovations from afar.

Tired Landlords

California's tenant-friendly laws, including strict rent control in many areas and complex eviction procedures, have left many landlords exhausted. If you have a rental property with deferred maintenance or difficult tenant situations, selling as-is can be your cleanest exit strategy.

Properties with Major Issues

Homes with foundation problems, extensive fire or water damage, code violations, or hazardous materials like asbestos or mold are often better sold as-is. The cost to repair these issues can exceed the value they add, especially when factoring in the time and stress involved.

What to Watch Out For: Avoiding As-Is Sale Pitfalls

While selling as-is can be the right choice, California homeowners should be aware of potential issues.

Lowball Offers

Some investors make extremely low offers hoping sellers don't understand their property's true value. Even with needed repairs, your Southern California home has substantial value. Get multiple offers and consider getting a professional assessment of repair costs to evaluate offers intelligently.

Disclosure Liability

Never think 'as-is' means you can hide problems. California courts consistently side with buyers in disclosure disputes. Always complete all required disclosures honestly and thoroughly. Consult with a real estate attorney if you're unsure about disclosure obligations for specific issues.

Unethical Buyers

Some buyers use aggressive tactics, create false urgency, or attempt to renegotiate dramatically right before closing. Work with established, reputable companies with verifiable track records in California. Check reviews, verify licensing, and don't hesitate to walk away from buyers who pressure you or change terms unexpectedly.

Tax Implications

Selling your home may have tax consequences, though California's high property values mean many sellers benefit from the capital gains exclusion ($250,000 for individuals, $500,000 for married couples). However, if you've depreciated the property as a rental or if the gain exceeds these thresholds, consult with a tax professional about your specific situation.
