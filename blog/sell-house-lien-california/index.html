Understanding Property Liens in California

A lien is a legal claim against your property that must be satisfied before you can transfer clear title to a buyer. In California, liens attach to the property itself, not just to you as the owner. This means they typically need to be resolved during the sale process.

Common types of liens California homeowners face include:

  • Tax liens: Unpaid property taxes, state income taxes, or IRS liens
  • Mechanic's liens: From contractors or subcontractors who weren't paid for work on your property
  • Judgment liens: From court judgments in lawsuits
  • HOA liens: Unpaid homeowners association dues (common in Irvine, Mission Viejo, and other planned communities)
  • Mortgage liens: Your primary mortgage and any second mortgages or HELOCs

The good news for Southern California homeowners is that property values in areas like Orange County, Los Angeles, and San Diego are typically high enough to cover most liens and still leave you with equity.

California-Specific Lien Laws You Should Know

California has specific statutes that govern how liens work and how they can be resolved. Under California Civil Code Section 2872, liens generally must be paid in order of priority, with property tax liens taking precedence over most other types.

Statute of Limitations

Different liens have different expiration periods in California. Judgment liens typically last 10 years but can be renewed. Mechanic's liens must be enforced within 90 days of recording for projects under $25,000. Understanding these timeframes can be crucial to your strategy.

Right to Notice

California law requires that you receive proper notice before most liens are placed on your property. If you weren't properly notified, you may have grounds to challenge the lien, though this requires legal action and time.

How Liens Are Discovered During a California Home Sale

When you sell a house in California, the escrow company will conduct a title search that reveals all recorded liens. This happens early in the escrow process, which typically takes 30-45 days in California for traditional sales.

Title companies in Orange County and throughout Southern California are thorough. They'll uncover recorded liens from multiple sources, including county recorder offices, courts, and tax authorities. Any liens must be addressed before escrow can close and title can transfer to the buyer.

Your Options for Selling a House With a Lien in California

Option 1: Pay Off the Lien Before Selling

If you have the funds available, paying off the lien before listing is the cleanest approach. Once paid, you'll receive a lien release that must be recorded with the county recorder's office. In Orange County, this typically takes 2-3 weeks to process.

Option 2: Pay the Lien From Sale Proceeds

This is the most common approach for California homeowners. The lien is paid directly from your proceeds at closing. Your escrow officer will coordinate this, ensuring the lienholder receives payment and provides a release before the transaction completes.

Given the strong property values in areas like Newport Beach, Huntington Beach, and throughout coastal Orange County, most homeowners have sufficient equity to cover liens and still profit from the sale.

Option 3: Negotiate With the Lienholder

Lienholders sometimes accept less than the full amount owed, particularly if the alternative is receiving nothing through foreclosure. This process, called a lien settlement, requires negotiation skills and documentation of financial hardship.

California's high cost of living can actually work in your favor here, as lienholders understand that financial difficulties are common even among property owners in expensive markets like San Diego and Los Angeles.

The California Escrow Process With a Lien

California's escrow process is designed to protect both buyers and sellers. When you sell a house with a lien, here's what typically happens:

  1. Open escrow: You accept an offer and open escrow with a neutral third party
  2. Title search: The title company identifies all liens (usually within 7-10 days)
  3. Demand letters: The title company requests payoff amounts from all lienholders
  4. Settlement statement preparation: Your escrow officer calculates exactly how much each lienholder will receive from proceeds
  5. Lien payoff: At closing, the escrow company pays lienholders directly and obtains releases
  6. Recording: The deed and lien releases are recorded with the county
  7. Disbursement: You receive any remaining proceeds after all liens, fees, and commissions are paid

California escrow officers are experienced with complex transactions. In areas like Irvine, Mission Viejo, and San Clemente where property values often exceed $800,000, they regularly handle multiple liens on single properties.

Costs and Fees for Selling With a Lien in California

Beyond paying off the lien itself, you'll face typical California selling costs:

  • Real estate commissions: Usually 5-6% of the sale price (not applicable with cash buyers)
  • Title insurance: Typically $1,000-$3,000 depending on property value
  • Escrow fees: Usually 1-2% of the sale price, split with the buyer
  • County transfer tax: $1.10 per $1,000 of sale price in most California counties
  • HOA transfer fees: Common in Orange County communities, typically $200-$500
  • Lien release recording fees: Usually $50-$100 per lien

With Southern California's high property values, these costs represent a smaller percentage of your proceeds compared to other markets, but they still add up quickly on a traditional sale.

Special Considerations for Southern California Sellers

Property Tax Liens in California

California's Proposition 13 keeps property tax increases low for long-term owners, but unpaid property taxes still result in liens that take priority over all other claims. Orange County property taxes become delinquent on June 30th if the second installment isn't paid, and the county can sell a tax deed after five years.

If you're behind on property taxes in Anaheim, Santa Ana, Fullerton, or anywhere in California, the escrow process will ensure these are paid first from your proceeds.

HOA Liens in Planned Communities

Many Southern California communities, especially in Irvine, Lake Forest, Aliso Viejo, and Laguna Hills, have homeowners associations with significant dues and strict enforcement. Under California Civil Code Section 5650, HOAs can place liens for unpaid dues and even foreclose.

HOA liens must be satisfied at closing, and you'll also need to pay any transfer fees and provide required documents to the new owner.

Mechanic's Liens From Contractors

California's construction industry is robust, and mechanic's liens are common when contractors aren't paid. Under California Civil Code Section 8400, contractors, subcontractors, and material suppliers can file liens within 90 days of project completion.

If you dispute a mechanic's lien, you can record a bond in the amount of 125% of the lien claim, which removes the lien from your title while the dispute is resolved.
