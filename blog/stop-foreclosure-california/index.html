How to Stop Foreclosure in California - Your Options

If you're facing foreclosure in Southern California, you're not alone—and you have more options than you might think. This guide will walk you through practical steps to stop foreclosure in California and protect your home equity.

Understanding California's Foreclosure Process

California is primarily a non-judicial foreclosure state, meaning most foreclosures happen without court involvement. This process is generally faster than judicial foreclosures in other states, but it also provides you with specific rights and timelines to take action.

The typical California foreclosure timeline includes:

  • Missed Payments: After you fall behind on mortgage payments, your lender will typically wait until you're 90-120 days delinquent before initiating foreclosure
  • Notice of Default (NOD): This is the first official foreclosure notice, giving you at least 90 days to catch up on payments
  • Notice of Trustee Sale (NTS): Filed at least 20 days before the scheduled auction date
  • Trustee Sale: Your property is sold at public auction, typically on courthouse steps

From the Notice of Default to the actual sale, you have a minimum of 111 days in California—but acting sooner gives you more options.

8 Ways to Stop Foreclosure in California

1. Reinstate Your Loan

California law gives you the right to reinstate your mortgage by paying all past-due amounts, plus fees and costs, up until five business days before the foreclosure sale. This is one of the most straightforward ways to stop foreclosure if you've come into money or can access funds.

In Southern California's high-value housing market, where median home prices in Orange County exceed $1 million, the reinstatement amount can be substantial. Contact your lender immediately to get an exact payoff quote.

2. Apply for a Loan Modification

Under California's Homeowner Bill of Rights (Civil Code Section 2923.6), lenders must stop foreclosure proceedings if you submit a complete loan modification application at least five business days before a scheduled sale. This law provides critical breathing room while you negotiate new terms.

Loan modifications can include:

  • Reduced interest rates
  • Extended loan terms
  • Principal forbearance
  • Conversion from adjustable to fixed rates

Southern California lenders are often willing to modify loans given the region's strong long-term real estate market fundamentals.

3. File for Bankruptcy

Filing for Chapter 7 or Chapter 13 bankruptcy triggers an automatic stay that immediately halts foreclosure proceedings. While this is a serious step with long-term credit implications, it can provide time to reorganize your finances.

Chapter 13 bankruptcy allows you to catch up on missed payments over 3-5 years while keeping your home. This option works well if you have steady income but fell behind due to temporary hardship.

4. Sell Your Home Quickly for Cash

If you have equity in your Southern California property—which many homeowners do given the region's appreciation over the past decade—selling quickly can help you avoid foreclosure, preserve your credit, and walk away with cash.

Traditional listings in Orange County average 30-60 days on market, plus 30-45 days for escrow. When facing foreclosure, you may not have that much time. Cash buyers can close in as little as 7-14 days, well before your trustee sale date.

This option makes particular sense if:

  • You have equity but can't afford the payments
  • You need to relocate for work
  • You've inherited a property you can't maintain
  • The home needs repairs you can't afford

5. Request a Forbearance Agreement

A forbearance temporarily suspends or reduces your mortgage payments while you recover from financial hardship. California homeowners affected by job loss, medical issues, or other hardships may qualify.

Post-pandemic, many lenders have expanded forbearance programs. Contact your servicer immediately to discuss options—the key is communicating before you fall too far behind.

6. Pursue a Short Sale

If you owe more than your home is worth (underwater), a short sale allows you to sell for less than the mortgage balance with lender approval. While less common in Southern California's strong market, some homeowners who purchased at peak prices may find themselves in this position.

California's anti-deficiency laws (Code of Civil Procedure Section 580e) protect you from owing the difference on most owner-occupied properties, making short sales less risky here than in other states.

7. Challenge the Foreclosure

You have the right to dispute a foreclosure if your lender violated California's strict procedural requirements. Common grounds for challenge include:

  • Failure to properly send the Notice of Default
  • Not providing contact information for housing counseling agencies
  • Proceeding with foreclosure while a loan modification is pending
  • Errors in the amount claimed due

California's Homeowner Bill of Rights provides significant protections. Consulting with a foreclosure attorney can help identify if your lender made violations.

8. Deed in Lieu of Foreclosure

As a last resort, you can voluntarily transfer your property deed to the lender in exchange for release from the mortgage. This avoids foreclosure on your record and is less damaging to your credit than foreclosure, though it still has significant impact.

Lenders may offer cash-for-keys incentives (typically $2,000-$5,000) to leave the property in good condition.

California-Specific Foreclosure Protections

California homeowners benefit from some of the nation's strongest foreclosure protections:

Homeowner Bill of Rights: Requires lenders to assign a single point of contact, restricts dual-tracking (pursuing foreclosure while considering loss mitigation), and mandates proper documentation.

Right to Reinstate: You can stop foreclosure up to five business days before the sale by paying what's owed.

Anti-Deficiency Protection: For purchase-money loans on owner-occupied properties, lenders cannot pursue you for the deficiency after foreclosure (Code of Civil Procedure Section 580b).

Tenant Protections: If your foreclosed property has tenants, California law provides them with minimum notice periods and, in some cases, the right to remain through their lease term.

What to Do Right Now

If you're facing foreclosure in California, take these immediate steps:

  1. Know your timeline: Find out exactly when your Notice of Default was filed and when the trustee sale is scheduled
  2. Contact your lender: Ask about loss mitigation options including forbearance and loan modification
  3. Get free counseling: HUD-approved housing counselors can help you understand your options at no cost
  4. Assess your equity: Determine how much your home is worth versus what you owe—this tells you if selling makes sense
  5. Consider selling: If you have equity and can't afford payments, selling preserves your credit and puts cash in your pocket
  6. Consult professionals: Speak with a foreclosure attorney, housing counselor, or reputable cash buyer to understand all options
